# Dealcraft 1.1 — the night the numbers started talking back

*19 September 2026*

Dealcraft automates the parts of Schedule I that are bookkeeping rather than
play: answering a customer's offer with a better price, accepting deals into the
hours you actually sit down to play, and filling in the handover while you walk
past. It has been installable for two days. For most of that time the headline
feature did not work at all, and nothing in the mod was able to say so.

This release is mostly the story of fixing that, and the fix that mattered most
was not a feature. It was making the mod write down what it saw.

## The feature that never fired

Price negotiation is the reason Dealcraft exists. A customer texts you an offer;
the mod works out the most they would still say yes to and counters with that.

For four sessions it answered the same thing about every customer: *no price at
any quantity reached the confidence you asked for.* Every offer scored zero. The
switch was on, the code ran, and nothing ever went out.

Nothing in the log said why, because the code that asked the game had one line
in it that swallowed every failure and returned zero. A refusal and a crash
looked identical, and both looked like a customer saying no.

So the first change was not to the negotiation. It was to make it keep a record:
what it handed the game, what came back, how many prices it tried, and what went
wrong if anything did. One line per negotiation, into a file you can read after
playing.

The next session that line said this:

> handed in 1 × Trio mit 4 Fäusten at 1 per package, quality Standard,
> **no packaging applied**; 14 price points, first 1 for $1000000 answered 0,
> last 1 for **$0 answered 0**, best anywhere 0; nothing threw

Two things settle it at a glance. *Nothing threw* — the game was answering
honestly, there was no crash hiding behind the zero. And **zero at zero
dollars**: a customer turning down a free product is not a customer refusing a
price. The offer was not an offer.

The mod had been asking what customers would pay for loose, unpackaged product.
Nobody takes that at any price. The handover code had known this all along — it
skips unpackaged stock when filling a contract, because that is not a thing you
can hand anybody. The negotiation was asking a question the rest of the mod
already knew the answer to.

One fix, and the same line came back with real figures:

> Mrs. Ming: countering at $167 would be taken 50% of the time, under the 90%
> you set, so the offer on the table stands — packaged as Baggie; 155 price
> points, last 1 for $119 answered **0.99**

Real probabilities. A real curve over 155 prices. And a decision that holds its
fire for a reason the player set.

## The setting that could only ever say no

That was not the end of it, and the next problem was a design mistake rather
than a bug.

*Never offer below this chance* looks like it steers the search. It did not. The
search found the offer worth the most money, and only afterwards was that answer
held against your setting and thrown away if it fell short.

Expected value peaks in the middle of a probability curve, so the winner sat at
about half confidence every single time — four of one session's six negotiations
read *would be taken 50% of the time*. Lowering the setting from 90% to 70%
changed nothing, because the winner was still at 50%.

Your number now constrains the search instead of vetoing its answer. It looks
only at confidences at or above your setting, and takes the best-paying of
those — the best price **for** 90%, which is what the control always claimed to
be.

## The listed price is not a price anyone pays

Then a harder question: what should a product be listed at? The mod had a
feature for it that had never once completed.

The answer turned out to change the whole feature, and it is worth stating
plainly because most advice you will read about Schedule I pricing is a rough
approximation of it.

**Raising your listed price does not earn you more money.** A customer works out
how many units to order by dividing what they are willing to spend by your
price. Double the price and they order half as much. What they pay is set by
their own budget and how much they like the product — not by your listing.

What the listed price decides is whether they order **at all**. Past a certain
point relative to the product's suggested price they stop texting you. There is
no refusal and no message: they simply go quiet, and their orders migrate to
whichever of your products is cheaper. How far past depends on how much they
like it, which is why no single multiplier has ever been the right answer and
why the community argues about it endlessly. The three numbers you will see
quoted — ×1.2, ×1.4, ×1.6 — are three different customers, not three opinions.

So Dealcraft prices each product at the highest figure where none of your
customers go quiet, worked out per customer from how much they like it. In one
session that moved a product from $150 to $151 and another from $260 to $250,
and kept all 32 customers on both. The version before had been managing twelve.

There is a floor under it, at the product's suggested price, and it is there
because of a question the repo owner asked before it could bite: without one,
a single customer who barely likes the product sets the price for everybody, and
the next such customer sets it lower again. Below the suggested price nothing is
gained anyway — acceptance is already at its limit there, and a cheaper listing
only hands out more stock for the same money.

## What the handover is actually worth

The handover is the part that worked earliest, and it is now the part with the
most evidence behind it.

Dealcraft keeps a ledger of what the game actually paid, separately from what
the contract promised. Over 54 completed handovers in one session:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| What the contracts promised | **$31,520** |
| What the game actually paid | **$40,337** |
| Bonuses on top | **$8,817 — 28%** |
| Customer satisfaction | **1.0 on every single one** |

That 28% is mostly one behaviour: where the contract asks for Standard and the
best thing in your pocket is Heavenly, Dealcraft hands over the Heavenly. The
bonus grows with every grade you exceed. Delivering exactly what was asked for
is still an option and some players will want it, but it is no longer what a
fresh install does — for one session that default refused every handover the
owner had while fifteen perfectly good baggies sat in his pocket.

A handover also holds four **stacks**, not four packages. Counting packages had
been refusing contracts a player could complete by hand: five units asked for,
thirteen in the pocket, and a flat no.

## Every decision, and every non-decision

The thread running through all of this: the mod writes down what it decided
**and what it declined to decide, with the reason**, to
`UserData/Dealcraft/decisions.jsonl`.

That file is what found every bug above. Not the tests — there were 767 of them
passing while the headline feature had never once worked. Tests hold code to
what its author expected. The file holds it to what the game did.

Some of what it found was not the mod's fault at all, which is just as useful:
nine handovers in one session were refused because the contracts belonged to
hired dealers, something the game says plainly and the player could not see.

## Getting it

Two files, two folders — `Dealcraft.dll` into `Mods`, `Dealcraft.Core.dll` into
`UserLibs`. Putting both into `Mods` is the common mistake and the mod will not
load. Full instructions, including a block you can paste into a coding agent,
are in the [README](../README.md).

**Upgrading:** clear the `[Dealcraft]` section from
`UserData/MelonPreferences.cfg` first. Settings are written there the first time
the mod runs and read back over the defaults for ever after, so without this you
carry old switches — and keys that no longer exist — into the new version.

Every automation switch ships off. Nothing acts until you turn it on.

## What is not proven

This is the part most release posts leave out.

**No counter-offer has ever actually been sent.** The search now finds them, the
arithmetic is right as far as anyone can tell, and 1.1.1 fixed the last thing
standing in the way — but nobody has yet watched one go out and be accepted.
Until someone has, that is a reading and not a result.

Multiplayer is exercised for handovers and not for the rest. The host-only split
is deliberate: negotiating, scheduling and pricing act on one shared customer
roster, so they run once on the host, whether or not anyone else installs
anything. A handover is your own goods leaving your own pockets, so it runs on
your machine. Nobody at your table is ever required to install this.

And this project has twice had two careful readings agree with each other and
both be wrong. The deciding vote is always the running game.
